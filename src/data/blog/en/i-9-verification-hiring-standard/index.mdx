Every employer in the United States is legally required to complete Form I-9 for every person they hire, verifying identity and employment authorization within three business days of the start date. This isn't an HR best practice or a courtesy step. It's a federal mandate under Section 274A of the Immigration and Nationality Act, codified at 8 U.S.C. § 1324a(b), part of the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986.¹ ² It applies identically to a five person shop and a Fortune 500 company.

I bring this up because I've recently gone through onboarding at more than one hourly, entry level position, work taken to bridge a gap while I look for the right role in my field, where I-9 verification simply wasn't completed. No document review, no form, no record. This isn't a callout of any specific workplace. It's a pattern common enough that it's worth making the case in general terms, with the data to back it up.

## It's Not a Formality. It's Law, and Enforcement Is Climbing

Form I-9 non-completion isn't a gray area. Employers must review original identity and work authorization documents and complete Section 2 within three business days of an employee's start date, and retain the form for three years after hire or one year after termination, whichever is later.² Skipping it isn't an oversight equivalent to a missed timesheet. It's a documented statutory violation from day one.

The financial exposure is real and rising. As of the January 2025 inflation adjustment, paperwork violations carry civil penalties of $288 to $2,861 per form, and knowingly employing unauthorized workers carries penalties of $716 to $28,619 per worker, scaling with repeat offenses.³ ⁴ In early 2026, ICE also narrowed the list of "technical" errors eligible for a 10 day correction window, reclassifying more than a dozen error categories as substantive violations subject to immediate fines.⁵

Enforcement activity has followed the same trajectory. Notices of Inspection issued in 2025 ran substantially higher than in prior years, with federal field offices operating under quarterly worksite inspection quotas.⁶ Hospitality and food service sit alongside construction, agriculture, staffing, and manufacturing among the sectors that receive disproportionate audit attention.⁶ ⁷ None of this is theoretical risk for an employer that skips the process. It's an open door to it.

## Why a Missing I-9 Should Read as a Red Flag to the Employee, Not Just the Business

I-9 completion usually gets framed as an employer's problem to manage: their liability, their fine, their audit risk. That framing misses something. The employee is not a bystander to this process. They're the one standing in the gap it leaves behind.

Start with the paper trail. The same intake moment that produces the I-9 typically produces the W-4 and confirms proper worker classification, the documentation trail that underlies accurate pay, tax withholding, and eligibility for wage and workers' compensation claims.⁸ These steps often move together because they happen in the same onboarding conversation, at the same desk, in the same ten minutes. That's not a guarantee that skipping one means the others were skipped too. But when the one step you can actually see gets waved off, it's fair to ask what else got waved off in that same conversation, out of view.

Which points to the more useful idea here: I-9 completion is one of the few compliance steps a new hire can actually observe firsthand. It happens in the open, at onboarding, with a form and a document check. Most of an employer's other compliance obligations, like payroll accuracy, correct classification, wage and hour practices, happen entirely out of view. I'll say plainly that this is a heuristic, not a proof. A missing I-9 doesn't mechanically prove that pay is wrong or classification is off. But it's the one visible data point a new hire gets, and a reasonable person weights the visible data point they have. If the one checklist item that happens in plain sight gets skipped, that's information worth taking seriously about the parts you can't see, even if it isn't a verdict.

## The Standard I'm Holding

I've spent a decade building systems for a living: pipelines, data models, dashboards, where the job is making sure the underlying process is sound before anyone acts on what comes out of it. I hold employment to a version of the same standard. If the one compliance step I can actually watch happen doesn't happen, that's a red flag I'm entitled to notice, on my own behalf, not just the employer's.

So the standard going forward is simple: skipping it doesn't disqualify an employer outright, but it means trust has to be rebuilt on a much longer timeline, unless the compensation is strong enough to justify taking that risk on faith. Not because the form is the point, but because of what a skipped, legally required, three day checklist item tends to say about the process behind it. And because I'd rather hold out for a role where the fundamentals, the compensation, and the stability all get taken seriously, starting on day one.
