Every employer in the United States is legally required to complete Form I-9 for every person they hire, verifying identity and employment authorization within three business days of the start date. This isn't an HR best practice or a courtesy step — it's a federal mandate under Section 274A of the Immigration and Nationality Act, codified at 8 U.S.C. § 1324a(b), part of the Immigration Reform and Control Act of 1986.¹ ² It applies identically to a five-person shop and a Fortune 500 company.

I bring this up because I've recently gone through onboarding at more than one hourly, entry-level position — work taken to bridge a gap while I look for the right role in my field — where I-9 verification simply wasn't completed. No document review, no form, no record. This isn't a callout of any specific workplace; it's a pattern common enough that it's worth making the case in general terms, with the data to back it up.

## It's Not a Formality — It's Law, and Enforcement Is Climbing

Form I-9 non-completion isn't a gray area. Employers must review original identity and work-authorization documents and complete Section 2 within three business days of an employee's start date, and retain the form for three years after hire or one year after termination, whichever is later.² Skipping it isn't an oversight equivalent to a missed timesheet — it's a documented statutory violation from day one.

The financial exposure is real and rising. As of the January 2025 inflation adjustment, paperwork violations carry civil penalties of **$288 to $2,861 per form**, and knowingly employing unauthorized workers carries penalties of **$716 to $28,619 per worker**, scaling with repeat offenses.³ ⁴ In early 2026, ICE also narrowed the list of "technical" errors eligible for a 10-day correction window, reclassifying more than a dozen error categories as substantive violations subject to immediate fines.⁵

Enforcement activity has followed the same trajectory. Notices of Inspection issued in 2025 ran substantially higher than in prior years, with federal field offices operating under quarterly worksite-inspection quotas.⁶ Hospitality and food service sit alongside construction, agriculture, staffing, and manufacturing among the sectors that receive disproportionate audit attention.⁶ ⁷ None of this is theoretical risk for an employer that skips the process — it's an open door to it.

## Why This Should Matter to the Employee, Not Just the Business

I-9 completion is usually framed as an employer's problem to manage. It isn't only that. The same intake moment that produces the I-9 typically produces the W-4 and confirms proper worker classification — the documentation trail that underlies accurate pay, tax withholding, and eligibility for wage and workers'-compensation claims.⁸ When that intake step is skipped, the employee is the one left without a paper trail if something later goes wrong — a payroll dispute, an on-the-job injury, an audit that catches the employer off guard.

There's also a simpler diagnostic value in it. I-9 completion is one of the few compliance steps a new hire can actually observe firsthand — it happens in the open, at onboarding, with a form and a document check. Most of an employer's other compliance obligations (payroll accuracy, correct classification, wage-and-hour practices) happen out of view. When the one visible, legally mandated, checklist-simple step gets skipped, it's reasonable to treat that as information about the parts you can't see.

It also says something about how a new hire gets placed. A verification step is, at minimum, a moment where an employer looks at who it's actually hiring before deciding where to put them. Skip it, and there's nothing forcing that pause — a person can be slotted into a role based on convenience alone, with no check on background or fit behind the decision.

## The Standard I'm Holding

I've spent a decade building systems for a living — pipelines, data models, dashboards — where the job is making sure the underlying process is sound before anyone acts on what comes out of it. I hold employment to the same standard: if the basic process isn't sound, I don't assume what's built on top of it is either.

So the standard going forward is simple: skipping it doesn't disqualify an employer outright, but it means trust has to be rebuilt on a much longer timeline — unless the compensation is strong enough to justify taking that risk on faith. Not because the form is the point, but because of what a skipped, legally required, three-day checklist item tends to say about everything else — and because I'd rather hold out for a role where the fundamentals, the compensation, and the stability all get taken seriously.
