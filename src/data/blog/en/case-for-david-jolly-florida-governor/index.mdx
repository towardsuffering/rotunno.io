On November 3, Floridians will vote in the state's first open governor's race since 2018. Ron DeSantis is term-limited. The Republican nominee is Congressman **Byron Donalds**, running with state Sen. Bryan Avila. The Democratic nominee is former Republican Congressman **David Jolly**, running with former Congresswoman Gwen Graham.

The race is closer than most people expected. Recent polls range from a 10-point Donalds lead (James Madison Institute, 52% to 42%) to a 7-point lead (NBC6/Telemundo, 48% to 41%) to a near tie (YouGov, 47% to 45%, and St. Pete Polls, 44.7% to 44.6%).

Let me be upfront about my bias. The main reason I'm voting for David Jolly is that I oppose the MAGA movement and the direction Ron DeSantis has taken Florida. Byron Donalds is running as the next chapter of both: he has Donald Trump's endorsement, "enacting the Trump agenda" sits first on his platform, and he has promised to finish the property tax repeal DeSantis started. That is where I'm coming from, and you should weigh everything below with that in mind.

What follows is my attempt to show that the case for Jolly holds up on the issues too, not just on who I'm voting against.

On economics, I don't fit neatly into either party. I am skeptical of tax-and-transfer as a way to fix inequality, and I am just as skeptical of trickle-down economics, the idea that cutting taxes at the top or across the board will pay for itself through growth. What I do believe is that free markets work most of the time, that they have limits, and that when a market fails, that is exactly where government is supposed to step in.

By that standard too, I believe Jolly is the better choice. Here's why.

## Two Candidates, Two Career Paths

**David Jolly** is a fifth-generation Floridian and an attorney who represented Pinellas County in Congress as a Republican from 2014 to 2017. In Congress he was known for working across the aisle and for the STOP Act, a bill that would have barred members of Congress from personally soliciting campaign contributions. He left the Republican Party after leaving office and registered as a Democrat in 2025 to run for governor. He won the August 18 Democratic primary with about 61% of the vote.

**Byron Donalds** has represented Southwest Florida in Congress since 2021 and won the August 18 Republican primary with about 48% of the vote. He has President Trump's endorsement, and his campaign lists "enacting the Trump agenda" first among its priorities, alongside immigration enforcement, affordability, and repealing homestead property taxes.

Both are serious people. The question is not which one sounds more pro-business or more populist. It is which one has a plan for Florida's finances that actually adds up, and which one is focused on the places where Florida's markets are actually failing.

---

## What Jolly Is Not Proposing

The most important fact in this race is one that gets lost in the ads: **Florida has no personal income tax, and the Florida Constitution prohibits one.** No governor can create one on their own.

In September, Donalds claimed Jolly wanted to raise taxes on tips, overtime, and Social Security. PolitiFact reviewed the claim and found it wrong, noting that Florida does not tax income at all and that a governor has no authority to impose those taxes.

So when you hear that this race is about "taxing the rich," it is not. Nobody on the ballot is proposing a wealth tax or a state income tax. The real fight is about two things every Florida household feels directly: **property taxes and property insurance.**

---

## The Property Tax Promise Doesn't Add Up

Donalds has promised to cut and ultimately eliminate homestead property taxes. He supports **Amendment 3**, which the Legislature placed on the ballot in June (House 75–26, Senate 30–9). It would raise the non-school homestead exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028. It needs 60% of the vote to pass. If it fails, Donalds has said he would bring it back, and he plans to use the state's **Taxation and Budget Reform Commission**, which convenes once every 20 years and meets again in 2027, to pursue full repeal.

I'm a renter, so I don't pay property tax directly. I can see why eliminating it is an attractive selling point: for homeowners, especially retirees on fixed incomes, a smaller bill is real money every year. But a tax cut that is not paid for is trickle-down thinking at the state level: the promise that the money will somehow make itself back. It rarely does, and someone else ends up paying.

Property taxes raise roughly **$55 to $60 billion a year** in Florida. That money pays for sheriffs, fire rescue, roads, and, for a large share of every bill, public schools. The Florida Policy Institute, a left-leaning think tank, estimates that the $250,000 exemption alone would cost counties about $4.8 billion a year, rising to roughly $8.65 billion with full homestead elimination. Axios reported that eliminating property taxes would have cost Miami-Dade County alone $3.27 billion in a single year.

There are only three ways to fill a hole that size:

1. **Raise other taxes**, most likely sales taxes and fees, which fall hardest on working families, renters, and tourists' wages
2. **Cut services**, starting with law enforcement and fire rescue, which are the largest items in most county budgets
3. **Borrow or defer**, which is how governments end up with structural deficits

There is also a fairness problem. Florida's Save Our Homes cap already limits annual assessment increases on homesteaded property to 3% or inflation, whichever is lower. Long-time owners are already protected. Eliminating homestead taxes would shift even more of the burden onto renters, new buyers, and small businesses, which pay property tax through their leases and do not get a homestead exemption at all. Renters pay property tax too, just indirectly through rent. Homestead relief does nothing for us, and if local governments make up the lost revenue by raising rates on non-homestead property, rental buildings are first in line.

Jolly opposes Amendment 3. He has said he understands that "people are begging for property tax relief" and that he would respect whatever voters decide, but he will not promise to eliminate a $55 billion revenue source without a plan to replace it. That is not a left or right position. It is a responsible one.

## Insurance Is the Real Tax on Floridians

  alt={"Fort Myers Beach after Hurricane Ian in 2022"}
  caption={"Fort Myers Beach after Hurricane Ian in 2022. Storm risk is the main driver of Florida's insurance costs."}

  sourceUrl={"https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Damage_in_the_Times_Square_district_of_Fort_Myers_Beach,_Florida,_after_Category_4_Hurricane_Ian_struck_the_area.png"}

Ask any Florida homeowner what has hurt their budget most since Hurricanes Ian and Idalia, and the answer is rarely property tax. It is **homeowners insurance**. Premiums have climbed for years, insurers have left the state, and condo owners have been hit with both rising premiums and new reserve requirements.

Jolly's central proposal is a **state catastrophic insurance fund** that would take hurricane and wind coverage out of private homeowners policies and cover it through a statewide pool. His campaign estimates this could cut homeowners premiums by 60% to 70%. The fund would be financed by a 5.5% tax on insurance companies, fees on real estate transactions, and existing local tourist development taxes.

Donalds has offered his own "Bring Down the Bill" package, which includes reducing a surcharge on policyholders paid into the Florida Hurricane Catastrophe Fund when the fund is strong. It is a reasonable idea, but it is a trim, not a fix.

The difference is scale. Jolly is aiming at the largest single cost driver in the Florida household budget. Donalds is aiming at property taxes, which for many families are a smaller and more predictable bill.

---

## Is It a Government Takeover? No.

Donalds calls Jolly's plan a "government takeover" and socialism. That charge does not hold up.

Florida has used state-backed hurricane risk pools for more than 30 years:

- The **Florida Hurricane Catastrophe Fund**, a state-run reinsurance fund, was created in 1993 after Hurricane Andrew.
- **Citizens Property Insurance Corporation**, the state-created insurer of last resort, was created in 2002 under Gov. Jeb Bush.

Both have operated under Republican governors and legislatures for decades, and Donalds' own plan works through the Catastrophe Fund. Pooling a risk that the private market cannot price affordably is insurance, not public ownership of the economy. Homes, businesses, and every other part of the policy stay private. This is the clearest case of a market reaching its limits: private insurers have been leaving Florida because catastrophic wind risk on a peninsula cannot be priced affordably house by house. Stepping in where that market fails is not redistribution. It is the role government is supposed to play.

Donalds' campaign has also run ads warning of a "$1,000 hurricane tax." According to reporting on the ad, the study it cites did not evaluate Jolly's fund at all. It examined a separate legislative proposal to expand Citizens' wind-only coverage.

---

## Where Jolly Falls Short

A case is only credible if it admits its weak points. Jolly has real ones:

- **His insurance fund is funded partly by new taxes.** A 5.5% levy on insurers is a tax, and some of it could be passed on to policyholders. He has also supported "combined reporting," a change that would raise corporate taxes on some multistate companies. These are targeted business taxes tied to a specific service, not a general transfer of income or wealth, but anyone wary of tax-and-transfer should hold him to keeping them narrow and dedicated to the insurance fund.
- **The 60% to 70% savings figure is his campaign's estimate.** It has not been independently scored, and any state pool must hold enough reserves and reinsurance to avoid post-storm assessments on Floridians, the same risk Citizens carries today.
- **He is badly outraised.** Donalds has raised roughly eight dollars for every one Jolly has, and Jolly has publicly asked national Democrats to help close the gap. With so little money, he cannot reach as many voters, answer attack ads, or explain a complex insurance plan as widely as his opponent can, and that is a real weakness in a state with ten media markets.

I still believe his approach is the better bet, but he should be pressed on each of these points.

## No Debate, a Lopsided Money Race, and Why Your Own Research Matters

There will likely be no televised debate this year, which would make this Florida's first governor's race in more than 50 years without one. The two campaigns never agreed on a format: Jolly initially called for 12 debates, Donalds accepted three, and in September Jolly declined them all. His explanation:

> "It is clear, given the tenor of the current campaign environment, including Byron's repeated lies and bad faith, that debates this cycle would not create constructive, honest conversation and only further stoke division, animosity and badwill."

I understand that decision. A debate only informs voters when both sides argue in good faith, and Donalds' campaign has already run a "hurricane tax" ad built on a study of a different proposal.

The money behind the race matters too. Through September 18, Donalds and his political committee had raised nearly **$130 million**, compared with less than **$16 million** for Jolly, roughly **8 to 1**. Donalds had about $45 million on hand to Jolly's $4.5 million, and of the 18 billionaires who have given to the race, 17 gave to Donalds. That gap means most of the ads Floridians see between now and November 3 will come from one side.

The money gap is real, but it is not destiny. The top spender wins most races largely because money follows candidates who already look likely to win, especially incumbents, and this is an open seat. Research on campaign ads also finds that their effect fades quickly: a 2011 randomized field experiment on Rick Perry's 2006 gubernatorial ads in Texas found that their persuasive effect largely disappeared within about a week. A lead built on ad spending has to be bought again and again; a voter who reads the plans makes up their mind once.

The cost is that voters lose the chance to see both candidates challenged side by side. So the responsibility shifts to us: read both candidates' plans, check claims against independent fact-checkers, and look past the ads to the numbers.

Florida does not need a governor whose top priority is a national political agenda. It needs one who can make the state's two biggest household costs, insurance and housing, add up.

Byron Donalds is offering a popular promise: eliminate property taxes, with no clear plan for the billions in police, fire, and school funding it would erase. David Jolly is offering a harder message: fix the insurance market that is actually driving Floridians out, and do not promise tax cuts you cannot pay for.

The Miami Herald editorial board endorsed Jolly this month. Sen. Rick Scott has been campaigning for Donalds. Polls show a race within reach for either candidate.

My vote starts with opposing MAGA and the DeSantis era, and I won't pretend otherwise. But on the issues that hit Florida households hardest, the conclusion is the same. For a voter who doesn't trust redistribution to fix things and doesn't trust trickle-down promises either, who wants markets to do what they do well and government to step in where they fail, I believe the stronger choice on November 3 is David Jolly.

Whatever you decide, look at the numbers, read both plans, and vote. Early voting begins before Election Day, and Amendment 3 is on the same ballot.
