Most of the money companies spend to reach customers now goes to screens: search ads, social feeds, video, and sponsored listings. It is measurable, it scales, and someone can run it from a laptop.

My job right now is the opposite. My company sets up in-home estimates for home services, meeting shoppers inside both Lowe's and Home Depot stores. I work the Home Depot side, walking up to shoppers face to face, and I'm the company's top performer in Northeast Florida across both chains. No targeting, no retargeting, no dashboard updating overnight. Just me, an aisle, and a stranger who has no reason to stop.

I'm also a data analyst, so I wanted to look at the numbers behind the screen side of the business and compare them honestly with what I see on the floor. This article covers three things: where the ad money goes and what it actually buys, why selling in person is harder work, and why that work still pays off.

---

## Where the Money Goes

Digital is now most of the advertising market, by any measure.

- **Globally,** GroupM's year-end forecast put pure-play digital at about **73% of all ad revenue in 2025**, out of a market of roughly **$1.1 trillion**. WARC, which also counts the digital revenue of traditional media companies, puts digital at **just over 81%**.
- **In the United States,** the IAB and PwC reported **$294.6 billion** in internet ad revenue for 2025, up **13.9%** from 2024. Social media alone took **$117.7 billion**, and search took **$114.2 billion**.

The stores my company works in are part of this too. The Home Depot, where I work, runs its own ad business, **Orange Apron Media**, and Lowe's runs **One Roof Media**, launched in 2021. Both sell ads on their websites, apps, and in-store screens. The IAB counts this "commerce media" at **$63.4 billion** in the U.S. in 2025, up 18%.

So the same building I work in is also a digital ad platform. The difference is which side of the screen you're on.

## What a Screen Actually Buys

Digital ads are sold on reach, but reach isn't attention.

- **Display ads** average a click-through rate of about **0.46%** across industries, according to one 2025 benchmark report. Open-web programmatic display often runs closer to **0.05% to 0.08%**. Either way, more than 99 out of 100 people who see the ad never click it.
- **Home improvement search ads** are some of the most expensive. LocaliQ and WordStream's 2026 benchmarks put the Home & Home Improvement category at **$8.33 per click** and **$90.92 per lead**, against an all-industry average of **$66.69 per lead**. That makes it one of the most expensive categories to advertise in.

## Where People Actually Buy

Here's the part the budget numbers hide. Most shopping still doesn't happen online.

The U.S. Census Bureau reports that e-commerce was **17.1% of total retail sales** in the second quarter of 2026. The other 83% happened some other way, mostly in physical stores.

Home improvement is even more physical. The Home Depot's own filings show online sales were **15.4% of net sales** in the first nine months of fiscal 2025. Most of the business still happens inside buildings like the one I work in, where people want to see the product, ask a question, and talk to someone.

So a large share of ad money goes toward reaching people on their phones, while most of the buying happens in a store. That's where I am.

## The Harder Game

That's the screen side. Now the floor. I don't want to romanticize this: face-to-face selling is harder, and it's grittier.

- **There's no targeting.** An ad platform picks who sees the ad. I have to read the person in front of me in a few seconds and decide whether to approach.
- **Rejection is personal.** An ignored ad fails silently. On the floor, a "no" is a person looking you in the eye and walking away. Then you do it again, as many as 200 times a day.
- **There's no undo.** You can't A/B test a first impression or retarget someone who walked past. You get one chance at each person.
- **It's physical.** An ad runs the same at 9 p.m. as at 9 a.m. I walk close to 20,000 steps a shift and have to bring the same energy to the last approach as the first.

A digital campaign is built so the placement does the work. You pick the audience, set the budget, and let the ad run while you sleep. That's the appeal, and it's a real advantage. But the work doesn't disappear. It moves. Someone still has to call the form-fill lead and earn the trust the banner couldn't. The hard part of selling, getting a busy stranger to commit, has just been pushed further down the line.

My job doesn't get to push it anywhere. I do the hard part myself, first, with every person. You can cut corners on work, but you can't avoid it. Somewhere in every sale there's something hard, and somebody has to do it.

Recently I had a slow couple of days. Nothing was landing. I took a break, came back, and closed a $20,000 sale on my last shift. That wasn't luck. It came from treating the next person as a fresh start instead of the eleventh "no" in a row.

## "It Doesn't Scale"

That's the usual knock on in-person selling, and it's half right. A campaign can reach a million people overnight. I can talk to one person at a time.

But every impression a campaign buys costs money, and when the budget runs out, so does the audience. The floor never runs out. The Home Depot rang up **1,637.2 million customer transactions** in fiscal 2024, about **4.5 million a day**, and that only counts people who bought something. Saturday mornings are a rush and Tuesday afternoons are a trickle, but people always come. That access isn't free, since my company pays to be in these stores, but what it buys is a spot beside a line of people that never stops.

So my limit isn't how many people I can reach. It's my work ethic, my time, and how good each conversation is. A process only moves as fast as its slowest step, and on the floor, that step is me. The way to grow this work is better conversations, and more people who know how to have them.

## Why It Works Anyway

So selling in person is harder work. Here's why it's still worth doing, and the research is on its side.

In a 2017 study in the *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, Mahdi Roghanizad and Vanessa Bohns had participants ask strangers to complete a short survey, either by email or in person, using the same wording. **The in-person requests were about 34 times more effective.** The participants expected the two to work about equally well. People consistently underestimate how much a face-to-face ask matters.

Trust points the same way. In Nielsen's 2015 global survey of 30,000 people, **recommendations from people they know** were the most trusted form of advertising, cited by **83%** of respondents. Neither study is about selling. One is about asking strangers for a small favor, and the other is about trusting people you know, and a rep in an aisle is neither. But both point the same way: a person in front of you is far more persuasive than a message on a screen, and most people underestimate that. People trust people. A banner ad can't look you in the eye.

## Screen vs. Floor

| | Digital ad | In person |
|---|---|---|
| **Reach** | Bought, one impression at a time | Paid for with a spot in the store, where the people never stop |

## What I'm Taking Away

Most ad money now goes to screens, and the numbers show why: screens are great at reach. But a screen mostly buys a glance, and most buying still happens in stores, where the people never stop coming. Turning them into something real can't be handed off to a placement. It takes a person in the aisle, every shift, doing the part of the work that never goes away.

As an analyst, I'm used to looking at a funnel as rows in a table. Working the floor, I live inside one: people walking past, stops, conversations, booked appointments, appointments that sit. Stops come before appointments, so I can tell how a day is going long before the appointment count shows it.

Data tells you where people drop off. The floor tells you why. I get to do both.
