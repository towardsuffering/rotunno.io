The music industry looks simple from the outside: artist makes music, people buy or stream it, artist gets paid. In practice, its revenue structure is one of the more complex in entertainment, with rights splits, intermediaries, and licensing systems built over decades and still adapting to streaming.

This article explains how money moves through that system.

---

## Two Distinct Rights, Two Distinct Revenue Streams

The starting point is that every song has **two separate copyrights**:

  src={fig4}

1. **The composition** (the underlying song — melody and lyrics)
2. **The sound recording** (the specific recorded performance of that composition)

These rights are licensed through different channels and paid out by different organizations. Overlooking the distinction is a common source of confusion about music industry economics.

## Performing Rights Organizations (PROs)

When a song is performed publicly, whether on radio, in a restaurant, on a streaming platform, or at a venue, royalties are owed for the composition. PROs collect those royalties and distribute them to songwriters and publishers.

<Figure
  src={fig1}
  alt={"An orchestra takes its bow in a concert hall"}
  caption={"An orchestra takes its bow in a concert hall. Public performances like this generate royalties collected by PROs."}
  author={"Dick Thomas Johnson from Tokyo, Japan"}

  licenseUrl={"https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0"}

/>

The major PROs in the U.S.:
- **ASCAP** (American Society of Composers, Authors and Publishers)
- **BMI** (Broadcast Music, Inc.)
- **SESAC** (a smaller, selective membership organization)
- **GMR** (Global Music Rights, a smaller invitation-only organization)

A related organization, **SoundExchange**, is not a PRO but collects digital performance royalties for *sound recordings* from satellite radio, internet radio, and similar services.

Here's how it works:

1. A radio station, streaming service, or venue pays a **blanket license** fee to each PRO
2. The PRO tracks performances (using statistical sampling, fingerprinting, and direct reporting)
3. Royalties are split between the **songwriter** and the **publisher**, typically 50/50

For a song with significant airplay, PRO income can exceed streaming income, particularly for songwriters whose songs are recorded by major artists.

## Sound Recording Royalties

The sound recording side is separate. When a streaming service plays a recording, it owes **master royalties**, typically paid to the record label, which then pays the artist according to their contract.

### The streaming math

Spotify's effective per-stream payout to rights holders is commonly estimated at **$0.003 to $0.005**, varying by country, subscription type, and contract. At scale:

- 1,000 streams → ~$3–5
- 1,000,000 streams → ~$3,000–5,000
- 1,000,000,000 streams → ~$3–5 million (before label splits)

Those figures are gross amounts before label splits. A traditional **major label deal** often pays the artist roughly 15 to 20% of recorded music income. An **independent deal** or self-release can pay 80 to 100%, minus distributor fees that vary widely.

### Label vs. independent math

| Scenario | Streams | Gross Payout | Artist Cut | Artist Receives |
|----------|---------|-------------|------------|-----------------|
| Major label | 100M | $350,000 | 18% | $63,000 |
| Indie (self-release) | 100M | $350,000 | 85% | $297,500 |

The percentage gap is large, but the comparison assumes equal stream counts. Major labels typically provide advances, marketing, and distribution scale that can increase total streams, which is the tradeoff artists weigh when choosing between the two models.

## Sync Licensing

When music is used in film, TV, commercials, or games, the production company pays a **sync fee**, negotiated directly with the rights holders.

Sync licensing has two components:
1. **Sync license**: rights to use the composition (paid to publisher/songwriter)
2. **Master license**: rights to use the specific recording (paid to label/artist)

A national TV commercial placement can pay **$50,000 to $500,000** or more, depending on the song and the usage. Because individual placements can be large, artists and publishers actively pursue sync opportunities.

## Live Performance Revenue

For many working artists, live performance is the largest source of income. A typical revenue breakdown at a live show:

| Revenue Source | Who Gets It |
|----------------|-------------|
| Ticket sales (net of venue/promoter) | Artist (typically 85–90% of net) |
| Merchandise | Artist (minus venue commission, usually 20–30%) |
| VIP/premium experiences | Artist |
| Sponsorships | Split with promoter/management |

For established touring artists, live can represent **60–80% of total income**. For new artists still building catalog revenue, it's often higher.

The live ecosystem also has its own intermediaries: booking agents (10–15% of performance fees), managers (15–20% of gross income), and promoters (who bear the financial risk and take a cut accordingly).

## The Streaming Payout Debate

Streaming has given listeners broad access to music at a low monthly price. How that revenue is divided among artists is an active debate.

Most major services use a **pro-rata** model: subscription and ad revenue is pooled, then divided according to each rights holder's share of total streams. A subscriber's fee is not paid only to the artists that subscriber played.

<Figure
  src={fig6}
  alt={"Streaming on a phone"}
  caption={"Streaming on a phone. A fraction of a cent per play only adds up at enormous scale."}
  author={"Shixart1985"}
  sourceUrl={"https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Woman_using_smartphone_while_listening_to_music_in_a_fitness_setting_during_the_day.jpg"}
  license={"CC BY 2.0"}
  licenseUrl={"https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0"}
  layout="inset-left"
/>

Critics of pro-rata argue that:
- High-volume artists and heavy listeners have outsized influence on how the pool is divided
- Artists with smaller, dedicated audiences may receive less than their own fans' subscriptions represent

The main alternative is **user-centric** licensing, in which each subscriber's fee is divided only among the artists that subscriber played. SoundCloud has used a version of this for some independent artists. Studies of its likely impact differ: a 2021 study commissioned by France's Centre national de la musique estimated that switching models would shift only a small share of total revenue.

Platforms have also adjusted pro-rata in other ways. In 2023, Deezer introduced an "artist-centric" model with Universal Music Group that gives more weight to artists listeners actively seek out, and in 2024 Spotify began requiring a minimum of 1,000 annual streams before a track earns recorded-music royalties. Supporters describe these changes as reducing fraud and low-value content; critics argue they disadvantage the smallest artists.

## The Full Revenue Picture

For a working professional musician today, income typically comes from a mix of:

1. **PRO royalties** (composition performance income)
2. **Streaming master royalties** (sound recording income, often via label or distributor)
3. **Sync licensing** (composition + master)
4. **Live performance** (fees, ticketing)
5. **Merchandise**
6. **Direct-to-fan platforms** (Bandcamp, Patreon, fan subscriptions)
7. **Publishing advances** (for songwriters with significant catalog)

Industry advisers commonly recommend diversifying across several of these sources, since reliance on any single one increases financial risk.

Understanding where the money comes from, and how it is shared along the way, is the foundation for evaluating any music business.
