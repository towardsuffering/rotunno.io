**Three children. One teacher. One pane of glass. Ground floor.**

On October 15, 2026, a 16-year-old shot through the glass panels of a locked door at **Silver Rail Elementary** in Bend, Oregon, and entered the classroom immediately to the left. Every victim was in that room.

The school had cameras, a buzz-in entrance, and new classroom locks. It even had a second floor.

The kids in that room weren't on that second floor.

## It keeps happening at the outside door

Locked doors are now the norm. In 2021-22, **97% of U.S. public schools** reported controlling access to their buildings during the school day.

Attackers get through anyway. At **Sandy Hook** in 2012, the front doors were locked, so the gunman shot through the glass beside them. At **The Covenant School** in Nashville in 2023, the attacker shot through the glass of a locked side door. At **Silver Rail**, the same thing. At **Robb Elementary** in Uvalde, an exterior door had simply been left unlocked.

The lock is only as strong as the glass around it, and the people relying on it. What matters next is what sits right behind that door.

## Change what's behind the door

Make the ground floor the front office, security, and the entrance. Put every classroom upstairs, behind one watched way up.

It wouldn't stop every attack. An attacker could already be inside, and stairs can be forced. But it would remove the setup at Silver Rail: a classroom one door from the outside. An intruder who breaks the glass finds the office and security staff, not a room full of children.

## The gap: outside

A building can't protect kids who aren't in it. Recess, P.E., ball fields, dismissal, and fire drills all put children outside the walls.

That has been the target before. In 1989, a gunman walked onto the playground at **Cleveland Elementary** in Stockton, California, during recess, with about 300 children outside. Five were killed. In 1998, two boys at **Westside Middle School** in Jonesboro, Arkansas, pulled a fire alarm and shot students and teachers as they filed out. Four students and a teacher died.

This is where security staff belong. Instead of standing at a front door the building already protects, armed security can be posted where the walls can't help: on the playground, at the fields, at pickup, and during drills. Play areas can also be fenced, or wrapped by the building itself as an inner courtyard.

## Schools are already being built around this

This isn't a fringe idea. Architects are already designing against attackers:

- **Fruitport High School** in Michigan spent **$48 million** on a renovation with curved hallways that limit a shooter's line of sight, concrete walls to hide behind, a corner in every classroom that can't be seen from the hall, and impact-resistant film on classroom windows.
- **The new Sandy Hook Elementary** opened with gated entrances, card readers, a staffed security vestibule, and classroom doors that lock automatically when they close.

Moving classrooms off the ground floor is the next step in the same direction.

## "What about fire?"

It's the right question. In 1958, a fire at Our Lady of the Angels in Chicago killed **92 students and three nuns**, all in second-floor classrooms. Fire-safety rules changed nationwide, and about 16,500 older schools were brought up to code within a year.

Codes have since made room for young children upstairs. **South Dakota** allows kindergarten through second-grade classrooms on a second story in fully sprinklered buildings with at least two exits straight outside. **Minnesota** allows them below the fourth story with sprinklers and smoke-detecting fire alarms. Building codes also let stair exit doors lock from the outside while opening freely from the inside.

Whether it fits a given school is a job for architects and fire officials. It also costs money: elevators, stairwells, and structure. Many existing schools can't be retrofitted. This is a standard for the next school a district builds.

## Who pays for it

Nobody gets a new school for free, so here's where the money already comes from, and where this fits.

<Figure
  src={figPay}
  alt="Bar chart: local districts paid 77% of U.S. school capital spending from 2009 to 2019, states 22%, and the federal government 1%"
  caption="Local communities pay for most school buildings. That's where this decision gets made."
  author="Rotunno.ai chart"
  source="2021 State of Our Schools"

/>

**1. Build it into the bond that's already coming.** Local districts pay about **77%** of school construction, mostly through bonds voters approve. Bend is a good example: Silver Rail itself was built with a 2013 bond, and voters approved more bonds in 2017 and 2022 that paid for safety work, including new door locks. A new school is going to be designed either way. Deciding where the classrooms go costs the least on the drawing board, before anything is built.

**2. Let states reward the design.** States already pay about **22%** of school construction, and many attach rules to that money. They could add one: extra matching dollars for new schools that keep classrooms off exterior doors and meet the fire code upstairs. That's how fire safety spread after 1958: a clear standard that new buildings had to meet.

**3. Ask Washington to match, not mandate.** Federal money covers about **1%** of school buildings. The main federal school safety grant, STOP School Violence, totaled **$83 million** in its 2025 round for the whole country, mostly for training and threat assessment. A modest federal match for secure design in new construction would help the districts that can least afford it, without Washington drawing anyone's floor plan.

None of this needs a new kind of tax. It steers money communities already spend toward a building that protects kids by design.

## The ask

When your district plans its next school or its next bond, ask one question: **does any classroom need a door to the outside?**

Ask the architects to price an option with every classroom upstairs and check it against the fire code. Ask your state whether its school construction match rewards secure design. Then decide with it on the table.

The kids at Silver Rail were one door from the outside. The next ones don't have to be.
