A murmuration of starlings looks like one creature thinking. It isn't. Researchers who filmed these flocks in 3D found that each bird tracks only its **six or seven nearest neighbors**, and that simple local rule is what lets thousands of birds turn as one.<sup>[1](#sources)</sup>

Organizations, markets, and customers work the same way. Big shifts are built from small, readable signals. The same instinct for what comes next shows up across everything I do, on its own terms in each place.

A sunflower doesn't plan its spirals. Each new seed grows at nearly the same angle from the last, and the pattern emerges on its own.<sup>[2](#sources)</sup>

Data behaves like this too. A single transaction tells you almost nothing. A few million of them, lined up in sequence and time, start telling you what happens next. My M.S. in Business Analytics from CU Boulder's Leeds School of Business trained that instinct formally: statistical methods, decision science, **predictive and prescriptive analytics**, machine learning, NLP, and deep learning. The math gives the instinct discipline.

## Forecasts Earn Trust by Getting Better

Anyone who lives in Florida learns to respect a forecast cone.

<Figure
  src={fig1}
  alt="Satellite image of Hurricane Frances, a large spiral storm, over the Florida peninsula"
  caption="Hurricane Frances over Florida on September 5, 2004. Forecasting is pattern recognition with real stakes."
  author="Tim Loomis, NOAA/NESDIS Environmental Visualization Program"
  sourceUrl="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hurricane_Frances_over_Florida_(2268-321).jpg"
  license="Public domain"
  layout="wide"
/>

In 1990, if the National Hurricane Center forecast a landfall near Tampa three days out, the average error stretched from Alabama to northern Cuba. By 2023, the same forecast's average error had shrunk to roughly Cedar Key to Sanibel Island, and 2024 set records for track accuracy.<sup>[3](#sources)</sup>

That improvement didn't come from one brilliant guess. It came from measuring every forecast against what actually happened and feeding the misses back in. That is how I treat any predictive work: a prediction you don't score is just an opinion.

For more than three years at AE Global Solutions, I worked inside **24/7 financial market data**, processing and validating 200GB+ a day. You learn the rhythms: how volume shifts through the trading day, and what normal looks like hour by hour. Once you know normal, abnormal jumps off the screen.

That is the core of anomaly detection, and it is why the platform caught problems before clients ever saw them.

## Dashboards That Answer the Next Question

A good dashboard anticipates what someone needs to see before they have to ask for it. I design them in the order people think: the headline number first, then what changed, then why, then what to do about it.

Hans Rosling showed how powerful that sequencing can be. In four minutes, he turned 200 years of health and wealth data for 200 countries into a story anyone can follow, because each frame sets up the next.

## Pages That Read the Way People Think

The same instinct shapes how I build for the web, including this site. Each section sets up what a visitor expects to find next: who I am, what I've done, proof that it worked, then how to reach me. When the sequence matches the reader's own questions, the page feels effortless, even though every transition was planned.

## Reading People in Real Time

Customers are patterns too. Across a career serving **100K+ customers**, you learn to hear where a conversation is heading: the pause before an objection, the question that means someone is already sold, the detail that matters more than the price.

In home improvement sales at Renuity Home, that read helped me earn **S-Tier**, the company's highest internal performance designation, converting homeowner conversations into scheduled in-home consultations at a rate that ranked among the top performers in the region.

That is the whole skill in miniature. Whether it is a chord change, a market open, or a customer's next question, the work is the same. Learn the pattern well enough to hear the next beat, then be ready when it lands.
